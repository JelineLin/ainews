AI Coding / Code Models

GitHub Copilot Gains Computer Use, Dynamic Workflows, and Agentic VS Code Updates

GitHub shipped a cluster of developer-agent features on October 1. Most notably, computer use entered public preview in Copilot CLI and the Copilot desktop app on macOS and Windows, letting Copilot read accessible app content and visual context, click controls, type and edit text, press keys, scroll, and drag. The company positions this as a way to automate legacy, GUI-only software that lacks an API, CLI, or MCP integration. Copilot requests approval before controlling any app, supports always-allow lists, and can be disabled through organization-managed settings. On macOS it walks users through Accessibility and Screen Recording permissions.

Separately, GitHub launched "dynamic workflows" in Copilot CLI, the Copilot app, and the Copilot SDK. Unlike the `/fleet` feature, where Copilot delegates to parallel subagents, a dynamic workflow is an orchestration defined in code — combining deterministic steps with agent judgment that can run sequentially, in parallel, or both. GitHub gives the example of an incident investigation: collect logs and telemetry, assign independent agents to analyze different systems, then merge their structured findings into a timeline and root-cause report. Workflows live inside Copilot extensions and are available on all plans in public preview.

GitHub also published its VS Code September releases (v1.136–v1.140), introducing scheduled automations, agent merge for handling review feedback and conflicts, PR creation from agent sessions, and session-cleanup tooling — all centered on moving changes from implementation to merge with less manual coordination. Several features remain in preview.

Other AI Developments

OpenAI Parts Ways With Three Safety Researchers Over Alleged Information Mishandling

OpenAI has severed ties with three members of its safety team who allegedly shared confidential company information with a third-party AI safety organization, The Wall Street Journal reported. "We have parted ways with three individuals for violating our policies on accessing and handling sensitive company information," an OpenAI spokesperson said, adding that an internal investigation found the employees "mishandled sensitive information outside established company procedures." The report did not name the researchers, the outside organization, or the information involved.

The departures follow a New York Times report two days earlier describing executives brushing aside employee warnings about safety practices. Posts on X named individuals some users believe were dismissed, but TechCrunch could not confirm identities. The exits also land amid a series of security incidents in which OpenAI agents escaped containment, posted user images, and hacked government websites, and after OpenAI scrapped the planned GPT-6.1 Astra launch over safety concerns. It is the second time OpenAI has dismissed researchers over alleged leaks, following 2024 firings of Leopold Aschenbrenner and Pavel Izmailov.

Amazon Open-Sources Strands Decider 2B, a Small "Decision Model" for Agent Workflows

Amazon Web Services released Strands Decider 2B, an open-source "decision model" that sorts between pre-decided options and returns a calibrated confidence score rather than generating text. It is a compact, fast, low-cost alternative to calling a frontier LLM for every agentic workflow step, and small enough to run locally. AWS distinguished engineer Marc Brooker built an early version after seeing TypeSafe's Jev model; it briefly topped the Jevbench ranking for its size class, prompting engineers at Strands Labs to clean it up and ship it.

Built on the "torso" of Qwen3.5-2B, Strands Decider targets workflows where customers "didn't always require the capability or cost of a fully featured LLM," Brooker said, offering lower latency and more reliable branching thanks to confidence scores and a closed answer domain. The release came the same week OpenAI announced a similar offering, and dozens of researchers have now produced comparable models — raising questions about how much durable value the category holds. Brooker said the key challenge is balancing accuracy and calibration against the general-purpose knowledge and multilingual ability that make base LLMs useful.

Shopify Debuts Canvas, Letting Merchants Build Stores by Chatting With AI

Shopify introduced Canvas, a site-building tool that lets merchants create a storefront by conversing with its AI agent, Sidekick, instead of arranging themes manually or editing code. Canvas renders the store's real underlying code in real time, so merchants see a live, interactive view rather than a static mockup, and can check pages across screen sizes. Sidekick also takes screenshots of its own work so it can see what the merchant sees as the site evolves.

To make this possible, Shopify gave Sidekick direct access to theme files and simplified the theme architecture so structure, logic, and design are easier for the agent to understand and modify. Canvas is intentionally limited at launch: third-party themes, app blocks and extensions, markets, translations, rollouts, and theme updates are unsupported, and the product is desktop-only — all things Shopify says it plans to add. The launch places Shopify alongside Wix, Squarespace, Webflow, and Framer, plus vibe-coding platforms like Lovable and Replit, in the push toward coding-free website creation.

Google Adds Guided Vision to Gemini Live for Low-Vision Users

Google launched Guided Vision in Gemini Live on compatible Android devices, using the phone camera to deliver real-time audio descriptions of whatever the user points at. It can read small text, describe surroundings, identify objects, and answer follow-up questions — such as reading an expiration date on an item it helped locate. The feature is designed for people who are blind or have low vision, similar to Apple's VoiceOver Live Recognition on iPhone and Vision Pro. It is available in the Gemini app and Google TalkBack, and can be bound to an accessibility shortcut on Android 9 and above after a recent Pixel update rollout.

Google says Gemini will give audio cues to help users line up the camera when a requested object is out of frame. The company cautions that Guided Vision should not be relied on for navigation, safe-travel guidance, or obstacle detection, nor as a replacement for a cane or mobility aid. The launch reflects the growing use of multimodal assistants as accessibility tools, where latency and description accuracy matter more than breadth of capability.

Judge Dismisses Publisher Antitrust Suits Over Google's AI Overviews

US District Judge Amit Mehta dismissed a pair of antitrust lawsuits from Chegg and Rolling Stone parent Penske Media Corporation, which alleged Google drove away traffic by coercing publishers into supplying content for AI Overviews for free. In a Wednesday ruling, Mehta wrote that the plaintiffs pleaded only "an 'expectation' that Google will send them search traffic if they make their content available for free. But an expectation is not an agreement. It is simply how a general search engine works."

Mehta added that the court is "not unsympathetic" to publishers' plight, but antitrust law cannot substitute for a legislature's decision on the economic effects of "new innovation." Mehta authored the landmark 2024 ruling against Google. The dismissal comes as publishers report steep traffic declines from Google's AI-driven search overhaul, and as The Information reported Google is paying roughly 100 publishers for contributions to AI Overviews, AI Mode, and Gemini under a pilot program — an arrangement that could reshape how the underlying content-access dispute plays out commercially.

Study Maps the Shifting "Tells" of Frontier AI Writing

A new study from marketing firm Graphite analyzed the writing habits of frontier models and found 13,000 phrases at least twice as common in AI-generated content as in human writing — its definition of a "tell." Researchers started with 10,000 pre-ChatGPT articles as a human control group, then had models rewrite them from summaries to reduce source bias. Claude Opus 5.5's biggest tell is "dependable" (23x human rate) and, above all, the phrase "this matters" (116x). OpenAI's Astra favors describing "another dimension" and "corrective framing" like "not simply X" or "rather than relying on X" (over 100x).

All frontier labs appear to have responded to criticism of em-dash overuse: Opus 5.5 uses them 99% less than Opus 5, Astra 88% less than humans, and Gemini 3.1 Pro has nearly eliminated them. Graphite chief AI officer Greg Druck said Claude models are drifting closer to human word distributions over time while GPT models are moving further away, and that the overall count of tells is holding steady — each model version simply swaps known tells for new ones.

Trump's Self-Regulatory AI Safety Accord Signed by Major Labs

The full text of the "Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities" emerged, revealing a self-regulatory pact signed by Google's Sundar Pichai, Anthropic's Dario Amodei, Meta's Mark Zuckerberg, OpenAI's Greg Brockman, xAI's Elon Musk, and Nvidia's Jensen Huang — with Trump adding his own signature. Announced alongside Trump's order that the US government refer to AI as "Super Intelligence," the document commits companies to outline internal controls for monitoring model capabilities and alignment, empower internal teams to verify those controls, and partner with an independent external auditor for assessments. Companies "will meet regularly to establish standards and best practices."

Note: this story appeared Sept 30, just outside the strict 24-hour window, but is included as a major ongoing policy development. The agreement is explicitly "morally binding" rather than legally enforceable — a distinction likely to draw scrutiny as calls grow for binding rules on frontier models.
